Writing progress reports for preschool-age kids is its own kind of hard. What do you even say about a 4-year-old? "Shows strong emerging scissor skills"? "Continues to develop as a snack-time citizen"?

Before I taught 3rd grade, I spent years working with young children as a Registered Behavior Technician, and progress notes were a daily part of that job. The lesson that stuck with me: parents of little kids do not want jargon. They want proof that you actually know their child.

Every comment below is built to do exactly that. They are organized by developmental domain, so grab what you need, swap in a name, fill in any bracketed details, and adjust the rest to match the actual kid. The same comments work whether your school calls these preschool report cards, Pre-K progress reports, or daycare and nursery progress notes.

## The formula that makes preschool comments easy

The strongest preschool comments have three parts:

1. **Something the child can do now** (specific, observable)
2. **The skill they are building next**
3. **A detail the parent would recognize** (the part only you can add)

Most of the comments below give you the first two. The third has to come from watching the actual child: the castle they built with three friends, the friend they look for at arrival, the question they asked about worms.

Preschool reports are progress reports, not report cards in the graded sense. You are describing a moving picture, not assigning a score. Keep that frame and the writing gets much easier.

## Social skills and friendships

Social growth is the heart of preschool. If your report card has a dedicated social-emotional section, our [social emotional report card comments](/blog/social-emotional-report-card-comments) go deeper on that language.

1. "[Name] greets classmates by name and regularly joins them during classroom activities. They are beginning to invite other children to participate in their play."
2. "[Name] participates in circle-time activities and frequently volunteers to answer questions or share ideas. We are practicing waiting for a turn to speak, and visual reminders have helped them improve this skill."
3. "[Name] checks on classmates who are having difficulty and offers to help during classroom activities. We are encouraging them to use words to ask classmates if they need help."
4. "[Name] often chooses to play alongside classmates rather than joining shared activities. We are using small-group games and guided play opportunities to help them practice interacting and playing cooperatively with peers."
5. "[Name] is beginning to take on different roles during pretend play with classmates. They are practicing sharing ideas, listening to others, and working together to keep the play activity going."
6. "[Name] shares classroom materials and often offers crayons, markers, or other supplies to classmates during art activities without being asked."

## Language and early literacy

7. "[Name] participates during story time by answering questions and making predictions about what might happen next. They can retell familiar stories using details from the beginning, middle, and end."
8. "[Name] recognizes their own name in print and is beginning to identify the names of classmates on classroom labels and sign-in materials."
9. "[Name] recognizes most uppercase letters and is beginning to connect letters with their corresponding sounds. Regular practice with alphabet games and letter activities is helping strengthen these early literacy skills."
10. "[Name] speaks in complete sentences and communicates clearly during conversations with adults and classmates. They are learning to add more details when describing experiences and answering questions."
11. "[Name] participates in rhyming activities and can identify words that rhyme. They are also beginning to create their own rhyming words during classroom games and songs."
12. "[Name] holds books correctly, turns pages one at a time, and uses pictures to describe what is happening in a story."
13. "[Name] identifies characters in familiar stories and can explain why a character did something. They are beginning to describe how characters' actions affect what happens in a story."
14. "[Name] is beginning to write the first letter of their name independently and can trace the remaining letters with teacher support."

## Early math and thinking skills

15. "[Name] counts aloud in the correct order to ten and is beginning to count beyond ten. They are also learning that the last number counted tells how many objects are in a group."
16. "[Name] sorts classroom materials by color, size, and shape. They can explain how the objects in each group are alike and are beginning to sort objects using more than one characteristic."
17. "[Name] recognizes and continues simple repeating patterns using blocks, beads, and other classroom materials. They are beginning to create their own patterns independently."
18. "[Name] uses words such as more, less, and the same when comparing groups of objects. They can identify which group has more or fewer objects during hands-on math activities."
19. "[Name] is learning to count objects by touching or moving each item once while saying the corresponding number. Practicing this skill during hands-on math activities has helped them count small groups more accurately."
20. "[Name] recognizes the numerals 1 through 5 and matches them to groups containing the corresponding number of objects during math activities."

## Fine and gross motor skills

21. "[Name] is developing a tripod grasp when using crayons and markers. They can draw recognizable shapes and people and are adding more details to their pictures."
22. "[Name] uses scissors to cut along straight lines with increasing accuracy. We are practicing cutting curved lines and turning the paper while cutting."
23. "[Name] demonstrates good balance when walking across a balance beam and standing briefly on one foot. They are practicing hopping on one foot and maintaining balance during movement games."
24. "[Name] is developing greater control when handling small classroom materials. They can pick up objects using child-sized tweezers and are practicing stringing beads and manipulating other small items."
25. "[Name] is making progress using zippers, buttons, and other clothing fasteners. They can now fasten larger buttons independently and are practicing smaller fasteners that require more precise finger movements."
26. "[Name] runs, jumps, and climbs during outdoor play with increasing coordination. They are practicing landing with both feet after jumping and moving safely around playground equipment."

## Independence and self-help

27. "[Name] independently puts away their backpack and personal belongings when arriving at school. They also gather their materials and prepare for dismissal without needing teacher reminders."
28. "[Name] is beginning to attempt everyday tasks independently, such as opening lunch containers and putting on shoes. They are completing more of these tasks before requesting adult assistance."
29. "[Name] regularly helps with classroom jobs, including distributing materials and cleaning up after activities. They follow directions and complete assigned responsibilities with minimal assistance."
30. "[Name] asks adults for help using words when they have difficulty with a classroom task. They are learning to explain which part of the task they need help with."
31. "[Name] can independently find their coat, put it on, and get ready for outdoor activities. They are becoming more consistent with completing this routine without adult reminders."
32. "[Name] independently follows classroom handwashing and bathroom routines. They complete these daily tasks without needing adult assistance."

## Work habits and approaches to learning

33. "[Name] begins classroom activities promptly and works until tasks are finished. During center time, they remain engaged with their chosen activity and follow through on the steps needed to complete it."
34. "[Name] tries different ways to solve problems during puzzles and building activities. When an approach doesn't work, they often try another strategy before asking an adult for help."
35. "[Name] asks questions about how objects work and what might happen during hands-on classroom activities. They participate in experiments and share observations about what they notice."
36. "[Name] is often among the first to try new classroom materials or learning centers. They listen to instructions and begin activities with minimal teacher guidance."
37. "[Name] is developing the ability to stay with challenging activities for longer periods. When a task requires extra time or practice, they are beginning to return to it after a short break or teacher encouragement."
38. "[Name] shares completed art projects and classroom activities with teachers and classmates. They are beginning to explain the materials they used and the steps they followed to create their work."

## Areas for growth

Every honest report names at least one skill still in progress. The pattern that keeps these comments from alarming parents: name the skill, describe the classroom support, and offer one thing to try at home.

39. "[Name] sometimes needs reminders to remain with the group during circle time and other teacher-led activities. Shorter activities and movement breaks are helping them participate more consistently. At home, brief songs or read-alouds with opportunities to respond can provide additional practice."
40. "[Name] is learning to recognize letters in their name and identify them in classroom print. Daily alphabet games and practice with familiar letters are helping them make progress. At home, pointing out letters in books and on familiar signs can reinforce these skills."
41. "[Name] is working on developing hand strength for drawing, cutting, and other fine-motor activities. Playing with playdough and using clothespins during classroom activities are helping them practice these skills. At home, activities such as tearing paper, squeezing a sponge, or peeling stickers can help strengthen the small muscles in their hands."
42. "[Name] is working on counting groups of more than five objects accurately. Regular counting activities with classroom materials are helping them improve. At home, counting toys, snacks, or other everyday objects can reinforce this skill."
43. "[Name] shares ideas during small-group activities but sometimes speaks too quietly to be heard during whole-group discussions. We are practicing speaking loudly enough for others to hear. At home, practicing a louder speaking voice while sharing a story or greeting family members can help reinforce this skill."
44. "[Name] sometimes watches or remains near an unfamiliar group activity before joining. Offering a familiar partner and clear directions has helped them begin participating. At home, talking through what to expect before a new activity or visiting a new place may help them prepare to join in."

## Comments for 4-year-olds

Four-year-olds are on the runway to kindergarten, so reports for this age can lean a little more academic while staying warm. These highlight the readiness skills parents are watching for.

45. "[Name] can identify beginning sounds in familiar words and is learning to recognize when two words start with the same sound. Rhyming games, picture cards, and listening activities are helping them develop these early phonological awareness skills."
46. "[Name] works with a partner to complete shared classroom activities. They are learning to divide responsibilities, take turns contributing ideas, and agree on a plan before starting."
47. "[Name] is developing an understanding of how print works. They can distinguish letters from words and are beginning to follow printed text from left to right during shared reading activities."
48. "[Name] follows directions for classroom activities that involve several steps, such as creating an art project or completing a building task. They are learning to organize materials and check their progress as they work."
49. "[Name] identifies common shapes such as circles, squares, triangles, and rectangles during classroom activities. They are also learning to use words like above, below, beside, and behind to describe where objects are located."
50. "[Name] uses drawings, letters, and labels to share ideas about familiar topics. They are beginning to explain what their writing represents and use labels to identify important parts of their pictures."

## Comments for 3-year-olds

Reports for the youngest preschoolers should feel even softer. You are reassuring the parent that their child is settling in, growing, and known. Keep the focus on comfort, curiosity, and play. These work just as well for daycare and nursery progress notes.

51. "[Name] has become familiar with our arrival routine and now separates from caregivers with a wave or greeting before joining classroom activities."
52. "[Name] frequently chooses sensory activities such as the water table during center time. They use cups, funnels, and other materials to explore pouring, filling, and emptying containers."
53. "[Name] uses sentences to communicate wants and needs during classroom activities. They are beginning to answer simple questions and describe what they are doing during play."
54. "[Name] joins in with familiar classroom songs and follows along with hand movements. They are beginning to remember and repeat words from songs during group activities."
55. "[Name] is spending longer periods sitting with the group during story time. They are beginning to look at the book, point to pictures, and participate when invited."
56. "[Name] is beginning to interact with classmates during play by offering toys and responding when another child approaches. We are providing opportunities for short, shared play activities with adult guidance."
57. "[Name] follows familiar classroom routines and is beginning to move between activities with fewer adult reminders. They respond to classroom signals such as the cleanup song and prepare for the next activity."

## End-of-year comments

58. "At the beginning of the year, [Name] often watched group activities before joining. They now participate more regularly with classmates and take part in shared games and activities. It has been wonderful to see this growth."
59. "[Name] has grown in their ability to communicate with teachers and classmates this year. They now share ideas more frequently during classroom conversations and use words to explain what they need. I have enjoyed watching these skills develop."
60. "[Name] has made wonderful progress this year, especially in [skill] and [skill]. It has been rewarding to see how much they have learned, and I wish them continued success in their next classroom."
61. "[Name] has been an important part of our classroom community this year. They regularly [specific positive action], and their contributions have made our classroom a better place. I wish them all the best as they continue learning and growing."
62. "It has been a pleasure having [Name] in our classroom this year. They have contributed to our classroom through [specific positive action or strength], and I appreciate the opportunity to have been part of their learning and growth."

## What not to write

A few things I learned the hard way, first as an RBT writing daily notes and later as a teacher:

- **No diagnoses or diagnostic-sounding language.** You may suspect something. The report card is not the place. That conversation happens in person, with support staff involved.
- **No comparisons to classmates.** "Behind the rest of the class" helps no one at this age. Compare the child to their own September self instead. If a concern is real, our guide to [writing honest behavior comments](/blog/report-card-comments-for-behavior) covers how to say it without softening it into meaninglessness.
- **No surprises.** If a parent learns about a concern for the first time on a progress report, the report has failed. Call first, write second.

## The real secret: notes you took months ago

Here is the honest problem with every list like this one. The comments only feel personal if you remember the actual kid: the tower they rebuilt three times, the water table afternoons, the first morning they waved goodbye at the door. And by report time, most of those details have evaporated.

The teachers who write the best preschool comments are not better writers. They just kept notes. Even one sentence a week per child gives you more material than you can use.
