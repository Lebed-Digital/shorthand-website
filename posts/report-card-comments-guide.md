Some comments are easy. The student who does everything right. The one who made huge progress. But then you hit the student who struggles every day, the one whose behavior took up half your year, or the quiet kid you realize you barely know how to describe. That is where report card season eats your evenings.

This guide pulls together everything on report card comments in one place. It covers what makes a comment work, then points you to the right set of examples for the student in front of you.

## What makes a report card comment work

Before you copy anything, it helps to know what a strong comment is doing. Almost every good report card comment has three parts:

1. **A real strength.** Something specific the student does well, not a generic compliment.
2. **One area for growth.** Described as an observable behavior, not a personality trait. "Is working on raising a hand before speaking" lands better than "is disruptive."
3. **A next step.** What you are doing, or what the family can do, to help.

Keep it honest. A report card comment should never be the first time a parent hears about a serious concern. If something needs a real conversation, have that conversation first, then let the comment reflect it.

And keep it about this student. The comments parents remember are the ones that sound like the teacher actually knows their child. Examples are a starting point. The details are what make the comment yours.

## Behavior report card comments

Behavior comments are the hardest ones to get right. Too soft and the parent misses the message. Too blunt and you have an upset parent in your inbox by morning.

[120+ Report Card Comments for Behavior](/blog/report-card-comments-for-behavior) is the main collection. It is organized by situation: strong behavior, improving behavior, ongoing concerns, impulsivity, attention and focus, peer relationships, and students with IEPs or behavior plans. It also covers the rules for keeping behavior comments constructive.

If you teach second grade, [12 Second Grade Behavior Report Card Comments](/blog/second-grade-behavior-report-card-comments) covers the behaviors that show up most at that age, like listening, transitions, and getting along with classmates.

## Comments for students who need extra support

These are the comments teachers agonize over. You want to be honest about the struggle without crushing a kid or putting a family on the defensive.

[Positive Report Card Comments for Struggling Students](/blog/report-card-comments-for-struggling-students) is organized by subject and skill: reading, writing, math, work completion, confidence, and working below grade level. The comments name the struggle and the plan, so they stay honest without being harsh.

For students with ADHD, [Strength-Based Report Card Comments for Students with ADHD](/blog/report-card-comments-for-students-with-adhd) focuses on focus, effort, and social skills from a strengths-first angle, plus what not to write.

## Preschool and Pre-K comments

Comments for a 4-year-old should not read like comments for a 4th grader. Preschool report cards usually follow developmental domains, and families of young children need language that is warm and clear.

[Preschool Report Card Comments](/blog/preschool-report-card-comments) has 60+ examples organized by domain: social skills, early literacy, early math, motor skills, independence, and approaches to learning. It includes separate sections for 3-year-olds and 4-year-olds, plus end-of-year comments.

For behavior specifically, [Preschool Behavior Report Card Comments](/blog/report-card-comments-behavior-preschool) covers routines, transitions, sharing, turn-taking, and emotional regulation in age-appropriate language.
