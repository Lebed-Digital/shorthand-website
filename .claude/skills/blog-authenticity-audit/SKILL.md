# Blog Authenticity Audit

Audit an existing blog post for writing that feels generic, automated,
overwritten, repetitive, unsupported, or disconnected from real classroom
experience.

The goal is not to prove whether AI was used. The goal is to identify passages
that weaken reader trust or usefulness.

## Core rules

1. Do not rewrite the entire article.
2. Preserve the author's existing voice and structure.
3. Flag only meaningful problems.
4. Do not treat polished grammar as evidence of AI writing.
5. Do not recommend fake imperfections, slang, typos, or forced personal stories.
6. Prefer the smallest change that solves the problem.
7. Separate factual concerns from stylistic concerns.
8. Do not invent personal experiences, research, statistics, or examples.
9. Flag claims that require verification.
10. Recognize that common phrases are only a concern when they are vague,
    repetitive, unnecessary, or used excessively.

## Evaluate

Score each category from 1 to 5:

- Specificity
- Natural voice
- Original value
- Conciseness
- Credibility
- Search-intent satisfaction
- Teacher authenticity

## Look for

- Generic introductions
- Empty conclusions
- Repeated points
- Excessive headings or lists
- Uniform sentence and paragraph patterns
- Vague claims
- Unsupported claims
- Corporate or promotional language
- Forced keywords
- Unnecessary definitions
- Advice that is obvious and adds no value
- Abstract writing where a concrete classroom example is needed
- Sections that sound unlike a working classroom teacher
- Abrupt changes in voice
- Formulaic transitions
- Excessive attempts to summarize
- Sentences that say little despite sounding polished
- Overclaimed developmental, behavioral, or child-psychology statements (see
  Overclaim check below, this is a distinct category from generic
  "overconfident statements" and needs its own pass)

## Output

### Verdict
Choose one:

- Pass
- Light edit
- Needs revision
- Major review

Give a two-to-four sentence explanation.

### Scores

Provide the seven category scores and a short justification for each.

### Priority flags

List only meaningful issues. For each issue provide:

- Severity
- Exact quoted passage
- Problem
- Why it matters
- Smallest useful change
- Suggested replacement, when a direct replacement is appropriate

### Repetition check

Identify ideas that appear more than once and say which version should remain.

### Verification check

List factual claims, statistics, policies, product details, or recommendations
that should be verified. Do not claim a fact is wrong unless evidence is
available.

### Overclaim check

Run this as a distinct pass, separate from the general "overconfident
statements" bullet above. This category has been missed by prior audits on
this site twice: a sentence can score well on specificity, voice, and
conciseness while still stating a sweeping claim about how all children,
all students at an age or grade, or all families experience something.

Flag any sentence that asserts a universal about a group of children rather
than describing a tendency, a common pattern, or this-author's-experience.
Two confirmed examples from posts already published on this site, treat
these as the reference pattern, not one-offs:

- "A third grader refusing to share is a discipline issue." (states a single
  cause for a behavior that could stem from many different things; also
  implies older kids don't have developmental reasons for the same behavior)
- "Saying goodbye to mom or dad is terrifying." (asserts a specific emotion,
  at a specific intensity, for every child, every time)

The fix is almost never to delete the claim. It's to soften an absolute
into a tendency ("often," "can," "usually," "for many kids") or to reframe
it as what the behavior often reflects rather than what it definitively is.
Watch especially for:

- A single word standing in for a diagnosis of cause ("is a discipline
  issue," "is defiance," "is manipulation") when multiple explanations are
  equally plausible (development, anxiety, communication, unmet need,
  environment, individual temperament).
- Absolute emotional or intensity words applied to a whole group ("terrifying,"
  "devastating," "impossible," "always," "every time," "no child can").
- Claims that imply one age group's behavior has an obvious explanation while
  a different age group's identical behavior does not, without justifying
  why the same behavior means something different at each age.
- Family-structure assumptions embedded in an example ("mom or dad") where a
  neutral term ("caregiver," "family") loses nothing and fits more readers.

List each instance with the exact quote, why it overclaims, and a softened
replacement. This check applies at every traffic tier, including posts
otherwise scored Pass, an overclaim is a credibility risk independent of
whether the surrounding prose reads as AI-generated.

### Strong sections

Identify two or three sections that already sound credible, useful, or personal
and should not be unnecessarily rewritten.

### Recommended action

End with one of:

- Publish as written
- Make the flagged light edits
- Revise the identified sections
- Rework the article before publishing

Do not recommend a complete rewrite unless the post has fundamental problems.
